# The grid operator already knows. The planning system has no word for it.

**Finding, 19 September 2026.** Source: Transgrid, *2026 Transmission Annual
Planning Report*, 191 pp, retrieved 19 Sep 2026, stored at
`disclosure-audit/transgrid_tapr_2026.pdf`. Not yet adversarially verified.

## The comparison

| Document | "data centre" appears |
| --- | --- |
| City of Moreton Bay's 16,519 development applications, 2016–2026 | **0** |
| Transgrid's 2026 Transmission Annual Planning Report, 191 pages | **187** |

One annual report from a network operator says it 187 times. An entire
decade of planning applications from a council hosting a $3bn data centre
campus does not say it once.

## What the operator puts on its own front page

This is not buried. The executive summary highlights panel, page 10, states:

> **66%** — "Data centre load accounts for **66 per cent of the growth in
> end-use energy consumption from FY26 to FY35**."
>
> **4×** — "Additional data centre load growth by 2035 **now forecast at 4x**
> 2025 Transmission Annual Planning Report data centre forecasts."
>
> **2029** — "minimum demand falls below 2,000 MW before trending back up **due
> to data centre load growth**."

Take those in order, because each says something different.

**Two thirds of all growth in NSW end-use energy consumption over the next
decade is data centres.** Not two thirds of industrial growth — of end-use
consumption, against a decade that also contains the electrification of
transport, of heating, and of industry. Everything else NSW is electrifying, put
together, is the other third.

**The forecast quadrupled in one year.** The same operator, the same
methodology family, twelve months apart: 4x. Whatever planning assumption
anyone formed from the 2025 report is wrong by a factor of four, and the
authority that produced both says so on its summary page.

**And the shape of the system changes, not only its size.** Minimum demand —
the trough, the hard problem in a solar-heavy grid — stops falling in 2029 and
turns back up because of data centre load. These facilities run flat, day and
night. They are not a bigger version of existing demand; they are a different
kind of load, and the network operator is planning around that while no
planning instrument records that a data centre is what is being approved.

## What the network operator discloses

> "In that time, Transgrid has received connection enquiries totalling **~20 GW
> of demand, roughly twice the current NSW current peak demand.**"

Twenty gigawatts. Of data centre connection enquiries. In one state. Against a
whole-state peak demand of around ten.

The report goes further than the number:

- **Where.** "Most enquiries seek connection in Sydney, with particular interest
  in **Western Sydney**, an area with substantial industrial zoning and power
  infrastructure." Also Riverina, Hunter and Central West "as proponents start
  to respond to network and other limitations in the Sydney metropolitan area."
- **How big.** "The industry's shift to very large facilities, at times adding
  **more than 1 GW of demand in a single location**, has pushed some data
  centres to seek **direct connection to the transmission grid**."
- **Quality of the pipeline.** "Only a proportion of these projects are expected
  to proceed to energisation as some enquiries are speculative or duplicated
  across networks." Transgrid commissioned Baringa Partners to build a
  NSW-wide forecast with Ausgrid, Endeavour and Evoenergy precisely to strip out
  duplication, and adopted its Central Scenario as the planning assumption.
- **The constraint it creates.** Under the National Electricity Rules the grid
  is "open access", meaning **Transgrid must process all applications to
  connect.** The report calls this "a challenge for transmission planning",
  because "multiple, large data centre projects often seek to connect in the
  same area and within relatively short timeframes."

Figure 2 of the same report — reproduced at
[`figures/transgrid-2026-tapr-fig2-supply-chain.png`](figures/transgrid-2026-tapr-fig2-supply-chain.png) —
shows **"Direct connect customers"** as one of three customer classes, wired
straight from a transmission substation, bypassing distribution entirely. It is
the operator's own diagram, and it treats direct connection as a normal
category rather than an exception.

## Why this matters more than any single site

Everything this project has documented about disclosure has been about what an
individual facility says in an individual planning file. This is the aggregate,
and it exists — just not where anyone thinks to look.

- The **planning system** cannot name a data centre. Queensland consents them as
  "utility installation" or "high impact industry"; the NSW discovery pipeline
  has to infer them from construction cost, which is why fifteen proposals had
  to be hand-rejected as warehouses.
- The **energy system** names them constantly, quantifies them in gigawatts,
  locates them by region, forecasts them to 2040, and commissions independent
  consultants to de-duplicate the pipeline.

So the question "how much data centre capacity is coming to NSW?" has a public
answer. It is 20 GW of enquiries, narrowed by a published forecast. It is simply
not in any planning document, and nothing in the planning system is obliged to
consult it.

## What it does to the earlier findings

**It confirms the siting hypothesis from the operator's own mouth.** The
[grid-siting note](GRID-SITING.md) proposed that siting is transmission-led.
Transgrid states it: facilities above 1 GW seek direct transmission connection,
and enquiries cluster where industrial zoning and power infrastructure coincide.
Western Sydney is named as the primary location, which is exactly where this
project's NSW discovery keeps surfacing applications it cannot classify.

**It reframes the value gap.** The
[investment-value finding](FINDING-investment-value-gap.md) showed only 6 of 94
rows carry a value and 8 an approval date. The pipeline is not unmeasured — it
is measured in the wrong unit, by a different institution, in gigawatts rather
than dollars, and nobody joins them.

**It sharpens the connection-agreement argument.** The
[sovereignty finding](FINDING-connection-agreement-sovereignty.md) argued the
connection is the sovereign asset allocated invisibly. The TAPR shows the
allocation has a queue, the queue is 20 GW long, and the operator is publicly
asking for "stronger commitment mechanisms for large-load connections" because
speculative enquiries are clogging it. The queue is public. Who is in it is not.

## The open question this creates

**Twenty gigawatts of enquiries. Ninety-four sites in this tracker. What is the
overlap?**

If the tracker's NSW rows account for only a fraction of 20 GW, then the
difference is projects that have approached the grid and not yet appeared in any
planning record, press release or portal — the pipeline before it becomes
visible. That is precisely the anticipatory dataset the grid-siting note
predicted, and it is now known to exist and to be large.

It cannot be resolved from this document alone: the TAPR gives an aggregate, not
a list. Whether a per-enquiry breakdown is obtainable — by location, capacity or
proponent — is the next thing to test, and a refusal would itself be a finding
about where the public record stops.

## Limits

- The 20 GW is **enquiries**, not commitments, and Transgrid says so plainly:
  some are speculative, some duplicated across networks. It must never be
  reported as approved or committed capacity. The Baringa Central Scenario, not
  the enquiry total, is the operator's own planning number.
- This is **Transgrid only** — NSW and the ACT. Powerlink, AusNet, ElectraNet,
  Western Power and TasNetworks publish their own reports, unread here.
- The term count comparison is between a *planning application description
  field* and a *report's full text*. They are different kinds of document, and
  the comparison is rhetorical as much as evidentiary. What it is not is
  unfair: both are the public record of the same facilities.
- Not adversarially verified.
